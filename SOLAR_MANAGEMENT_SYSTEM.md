Solar Installation Management System
Build the digital backbone of a solar company that has outgrown WhatsApp quotes and
site registers.
1) The Challenge
 Your task is to build one unified software platform that solves the real, day-to-day
operational headaches of a growing rooftop solar company. Think of it as the digital
backbone that takes a customer from the first quote request to the 20-year warranty.
Read the scenes that follow as things that actually happen on an ordinary week, and
design the system that makes each of them easy.
2) Meet SunPower Solutions
 SunPower is a fast-growing solar installation company that completes about 40 rooftop
projects a month for homes and small factories. It runs roof surveys, keeps high-value
stock such as panels, inverters and batteries, and sends a crew of 15 technicians to
customer sites. After installation, an endless stream of service calls follows. It sells
ready-made systems by capacity, from 1 kW to 5 kW, in two kinds: On-Grid (connected to
the electricity grid) and Off-Grid (with battery backup).
Packages
● Solar Panel Set (Hardware): Solar panels, connectors and wires

● On-Grid System (1 kW to 5 kW): Inverter (turns sun power into home electricity),

How Things Run Today
● Customer enquiries: Arrive on WhatsApp, calls and a web form, and get lost
● Quotes: Typed by hand each time, with parts often missed
● Serial numbers: Noted on paper, and lost by the time a warranty claim comes
● Site work: Recorded in site registers, with photos scattered on phones
● Revenue and margins: No clear picture anywhere
The owner is losing money, customers are frustrated, and technicians are
overwhelmed.
3) A Week at SunPower
 The scenes below follow one week at SunPower, from a homeowner asking for a quote to
the director reviewing the month. Every one of them is a problem someone at the
company faces today.
The rooftop inquiry
A homeowner fills in a form online asking for a 3 kW system under the subsidy. Their
name, phone, address and needs should land with the sales team straight away, and the
enquiry should never vanish.
Someone must call them and check the basics: how much shadow-free roof space they
have, what their monthly electricity bill is, and whether the grid connection is suitable.
If it looks good, a technician visits to measure the roof direction, tilt and cable distance.
Only after the survey is approved should the customer receive a quote.

Getting the kit right
The customer says yes to a 3 kW system. The quote should come from a ready template,
and the exact list of parts should follow on its own: panels, inverter, stands, switches,
wires and safety parts. Nobody should have to remember every small part.
A customer usually pays an advance, or the full amount, before work starts. Once the
order is confirmed, the warehouse should know what to pack and the installation crew
should see the job on their schedule, without anyone phoning to tell them.
Dispatch day and serial numbers
The truck is about to leave with ₹1,50,000 worth of inverters and batteries. Every inverter,
battery and charge controller has its own serial number, and each must be scanned
before it leaves the warehouse so the company knows exactly which unit went to which
customer.
The moment a delivery is done, the warranty clock should start by itself for every serial
number: for example 2 years for an inverter or battery, and 20 years for solar panels. No
one should have to type dates into a register.
On the roof
The crew mounts the stands, lays the panels, connects the boxes and finishes the
earthing. They test that power is being generated, then open a simple form on their
phone or tablet.
There they note the start and finish times, confirm the system size, and upload photos
of the finished roof. The homeowner signs on the screen to accept the work. The
company should have clear proof of the job, and the customer should have no doubt
about what was installed.

The rainy season breakdown
Eighteen months later, it is pouring and a customer calls: the inverter is showing a fault
code and there is zero power. Support should find the customer and the exact inverter
serial number within seconds, and see whether it is still under warranty.
If it is, the repair or replacement should cost the customer nothing. If not, the parts and
labour should be billed automatically. Either way the complaint should be tracked as a
ticket until the customer confirms that everything works.
The director’s month-end
The director sits down and asks a simple question: which installations made money,
which lost money, and what is still unfinished? Advance payments, stock purchases,
technician hours and unfinished steps for customers are scattered across notebooks and
phones.
There are also invoices to send, government subsidy paperwork to track, and
technicians to pay. The director wants to see how the company is doing today, this week
and this month, including the profit on each job and the repairs still open, and to share
these numbers when needed.
